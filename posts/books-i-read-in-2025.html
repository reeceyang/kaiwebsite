Books I read in 2025

January 11, 2026

I read 19 books in 2025. This is one fewer book than in 2024, but I feel that I spent at least twice as much time reading this year as last year. I think this is basically explained by two factors:

1) All my completed books of 2025 are from May onwards; in the spring I was taking a history class that required a lot of dense reading weekly and I did not want to spend my free time reading more.

2) I became much more okay with sampling and not finishing books instead of waiting for the "headspace" in which I felt "excited" to read a certain book all the way through. Mostly this means that I read a lot of books halfway through and then got busy and left them for later, which happens mainly if I'm consuming the book in online-PDF-whose-tab-I-close form or in standing-at-bookstore-shelf-for-three-hours form, so that it's not so easy to pick up a book I've paused on. However: honorable mention to The Covenant of Water, which I was convinced for some reason I would love, for taking up a whole torturous 2-week attempted reading period and two pounds in my suitcase over the summer before I finally gave up and pawned it off half-finished to a friend.

Thanks in part to #2 I feel quite satisfied with the set of books I've finished this year. Maybe because I haven't read so much and so intentionally since before college (!), and also due in part to some luck in book selection, my senses of what literature can do and of what constitutes a good voice (as in, one I enjoy) evolved more this year than any other.

I read 15 fiction books, 3 poetry collections, and 1 nonfiction book. I've ranked them in a tier list below, and I'll comment on my S-tier books. Like last year, I don't rank based on any supposed metric of objective quality, but based on the books' impact on me personally. I would say that actually I found the books in tier D rather odious and would actively anti-recommend them and only finished them to up my book count since they were really short, lol. But I liked all the other ones; for instance, two books in C tier made me cry.

Oblivion by David Foster Wallace

This was the defining read of the year for me: it changed my approach to my waking moments, my experience of my own consciousness, and my expectation for what writing should do at all. Thanks to Uzay for recommending a story by DFW in the first place, which elevated him from "vaguely pretentious writer I've heard of and will never read from" to, well, this, and Sabine for then recommending Oblivion.

Oblivion is a short story collection that I experienced as being themed around attention. Here is what I wrote about it when I finished (lightly edited for clarity; also reading this back it sounds very pretentious and my voice was obviously being affected by having just read 400 pages of DFW, but oops what can you do):

I don't have much new or insightful to say about the collection. But it did make me feel new things. Parallel to, or maybe in part spurred on by, my reading of the book, I gained over the past couple of months a distaste for abstraction, a salivatory impulse to treat life instead as a series of moments. For many years—at least since COVID, maybe much earlier—I had this impression of viewing my life through a 2D screen. Sometimes I felt genuinely shocked by the three-dimensionality of an object. I tried and failed to feel like I was inhabiting a real volume on Earth, or a tubelet through spacetime with four-dimensional richness, that could interact with other tubelets and alter their trajectories.

This is how time became abstracted away from the actual human experience of sensation after sensation: it calved into chunks allocated for this thing or that, and upon being spent, left no trace of the actual moments that had passed, only the label assigned to the sum of those moments, each of which I had barely experienced at all.

The idea of assigning value to different paths through life could also only be achieved post-integration, the summation and then categorization of an experience into "better" or "worse" than another, which is not wrong in the sense that there aren't experiences one might prefer to have over other experiences, but I mean that the categorization over and over makes different "paths" seem like discrete entities when they are not—are instead two labels for sums of experiences, only really useful after the fact as a point of reflection if at all, the entirely wrong thing to focus on either in the moment or looking into the future.

In the end all of this comes back to presence of mind, living in the moment, etc; but what DFW speaks to uniquely is the tension—more than a "craving for dopamine" or a "short attention span" or a desire for "instant gratification"—that makes such careful attention uncomfortable, at times painful or unnatural even, the very real and urgent tension between visceral boredom / sustained pain that it hurts to pay attention to, vs the desire to experience life. It was Incarnations of Burned Children [which btw is short and the perfect story and I highly recommend you read] that assured me he took it seriously, this hurt—that sometimes it felt as if the only way to survive was to peel one's attention back, at least a little, to compartmentalize, to detach, to avoid feeling the full thing, that this had not been invented by the modern brainrotted child but was instead a part of the Human Condition, a part to fight against if one wanted to live a full human life.

So this is what I thought about when I kept my eyes open the entire time while skydiving during Senior Week, watching the dull green horizon. And what I thought about during conversations where I usually would have avoided eye contact, sought the easy conversational path, presented only slight and easily surmountable disagreements or questions to the other person so they could feel engaged but not attacked, but now in really paying attention sometimes I found that people wanted that, and opened up to the genuine questions I had.

Pale Fire by Vladimir Nabokov

Thanks to Renee for this rec. This is a novel in the form of a dead man's final poem, a 999-line magnum opus, featuring commentary by his colleague. I think you need to read it more slowly, and intentionally, and probably just more times than I did in order to understand all the little connections. Even so, it was really funny, Nabokov's prose is cracked, and it was so fun to experience a novel in the form of "footnotes to a poem".

The real reason I've categorized Pale Fire as S-tier is due to the titular poem itself. I really had no idea that a long, fictive poem could be all of funny, moving, and beautiful to read. Some stanzas I felt I needed to physically read aloud, to experience the musicality on a physical level. It made me rethink the entire genre and was the direct inspiration for my first foray into poetry.

The Unconsoled by Kazuo Ishiguro

Thanks to Uzay for this rec. It's hard to describe this book: a pianist goes on many sidequests? Reading this felt like having a long stress dream and then waking up with a feeling of melancholy and tenderness and significance that I couldn't point to any specific reason for or communicate to anyone else. It also felt like some parts of waking life: making up your mind to change something and being frustratingly unable to enact this change in the physical world. If only a moment could arrive that throws your situation into sharp relief, lets you see its structure and change it, lets you take control over your fate. But you're just one person in gridlock with the rest of the world.

For all this it's also genuinely really funny, and made me laugh in public more than once. Lots of people claim it's frustrating, boring, or plotless, but for me it felt like a very smooth reading experience once I surrendered to it—the literal sensation of dreaming. It's the Ishiguro I've read that steps farthest away from what he does in The Remains of the Day and, to some extent, in all his other novels that I've read, which is constructing a portrait of a narrator through both what the narrator is and isn't willing to say to you, or even to themselves. In The Unconsoled, instead reality is warping around the narrator; we can no longer really apply the logic in Ishiguro's other stories which comes from an unreliable narrator living in a still externally-consistent/logical world. That was a pretty cool difference and I would be excited to see Ishiguro do something like this again, but my impression is that everyone thought it was a flop so maybe he won't.

Twice Alive by Forrest Gander

I picked up this poetry collection on a whim from a book sale like three years ago and finally read it in full this year. It's the first poetry collection I've thoroughly enjoyed and maybe the first collection where I feel consistently that even if I don't understand what the poet is trying to express in a certain poem, the beauty of his language carries me through anyway. Gander was trained as a geologist and I love his depictions of natural scenes (landscapes, biological/ecological dynamics) and how he uses them in his poetry.

Here's one poem I enjoyed—not my favorite from the collection, but maybe my favorite that's short enough to reproduce here.

Pastoral

Together,
you
standing
before me before
the picture
window, my arms
around you, our
eyes pitched
beyond our
reflections into—

("into," I'd
written, as
though there
swung at the end
of a tunnel,
a passage dotted
with endless
points of
arrival, as
though our gaze
started just outside
our faces and
corkscrewed its way
toward the horizon,
processual,
as if looking
took time to happen
and weren't
instantaneous,
offered whole in
one gesture
before we
ask, before our
will, as if the far
Sonoma mountains
weren't equally ready
to be beheld as
the dead
fly on the sill)—

the distance, a
broad hill of
bright mustard flowers
the morning light
coaxes open.
