Getting around Nairobi

April 6, 2026

Last summer after two months in Cape Town I visited my friend Sana who was interning in Nairobi. It was thoroughly dark outside on the first night when Sana and I walked to the Chinese food place we found on Google Maps. Upon approaching we saw to our surprise that it lay nestled in a modest Chinatown, a two-story complex of restaurants, markets, and leisure spots. We ate mapo tofu, cold enoki mushrooms with spinach, and bok choy with wood ear mushrooms. Only a few streetlamps lit the road. Without much of a sidewalk to speak of, we walked on the packed dirt between the road and the hitched-up canopies housing streetside operations, people grilling heads of cabbage or frying dough or hacking away at logs or heaving bags into wheelbarrows. The air was warm. People crossed the street the same time we did, between cars and motorbikes—bodas, they called them—that surged ahead regardless of what the traffic light displayed.

The next day I decided to head to the arboretum, a 40-minute walk away. I navigated the first leg of the route successfully, but when I came to the highway I had no idea which direction I even wanted to go in, having memorized the route simply as leading “across the highway” as if there existed the binary of “this side” and “that side”; instead, before me sprawled a sea of cars cutting across each other in every direction, people weaving between automobiles in this slow, deliberate, omnidirectional pattern, and beyond the asphalt and heads and car bonnets I just saw more heads and more cars.

My moment of hesitation was enough for a man on a boda to wave me over. “I’m going to the arboretum,” I told him.

“I know where that is,” he said. I got on the back of the boda and we lurched forward into the traffic.

It was my first time ever on a motorbike. The wind whipped my hair back and made my eyes tear up. For the first few minutes I kept imagining us careening into the roadside irrigation ditch or scraping up against the side of a car. Then I remembered I had no control over our destiny and relaxed and enjoyed the ride.

Forests in Nairobi are dense, filled with birds I can hear but not see. The canopy is this dark, lush green letting only stray speckles of light filter through. Both at the arboretum and while running through Karura Forest the day before, I passed by posters of demonic-looking monkeys that said DO NOT FEED. I don’t know if this is common knowledge, but Nairobi is filled with wildlife. It has a national park, so you can go on safaris in the city. Baboons sit on the side of the road.

But I hadn’t seen any monkeys at Karura, and after wandering around beneath the trees for half an hour I saw no trace of a monkey population in the arboretum either. I’d basically given up on monkey sighting and was strolling around the border of the arboretum when I ran into two Kenyan sisters around my age. They said hello, and I asked if they knew where the monkeys were. One of them, Irene, came to show me.

When we neared the fence on the perimeter of the arboretum I spotted the monkeys, silver-haired and smaller than I’d expected. They stretched out their limbs and combed bugs and dirt out of their fur. The smallest one finished eating a banana—letting strips of its peel fall to the ground one by one—and climbed over to investigate me, its miniscule hands gripping the branch and its face open but cautious, a meter from mine.

Irene asked me about the food I’d eaten in Nairobi, but I was vegetarian and she ate meat in every meal, so we did not really have shared experiences on this front. She showed me her bank account, which had the equivalent of three US dollars in it. “God will get me out of this country someday,” she said. She asked me to take a picture of her in front of some tall, thick bamboo and send it to her, since she said it looked like my phone camera was good. She’d come to the arboretum the same way she got everywhere, by matatu, the name for the buses, not strictly public transportation since they were owned by a couple big private companies. Matatus ranged in shape and size but nearly all sported dangling front fenders and garish spray-painted graphics so varied and nonsensical that I can’t hope to give a representative sample but will leave you with the image of a joint-smoking, gangster Donald Duck bordered by the words JESUS IS KING in epic font, and let you imagine all the other possibilities. Through the window I saw people packed in tightly as the bus puttered black exhaust all over Nairobi.

I got around by Uber. The matatus don’t have a route, exactly, they just go places and you get off when they are close enough to your place, so although they’re cheaper than Uber they are incredibly inefficient methods of travel. Scrolling r/Kenya revealed this was a popular opinion: people really just wanted a subway.

This lanky guy Joseph had driven me to Sana’s apartment from the airport and had given me his WhatsApp number in case I needed another ride. The first time we interacted—upon touching down in Nairobi—I treated him standoffishly enough to be considered outright rude, fresh off a couple months of literally everyone I met trying to squeeze money out of me in Cape Town. I yanked my luggage away when he reached out to help with it: two months earlier, landing in Cape Town, I’d been scammed almost the moment my suitcase entered my hands by a taxi driver who just took it and started walking with it, no “Do you need a taxi?”, just “You know the Uber app doesn’t work in the airport today because there’s a protest”—he had gestured vaguely in the direction of a group of people standing around in what I thought could indeed be construed as a poorly organized, halfhearted protest—“I’ve got the driver badge and everything”—he’d flashed it at me, already walking briskly, me following, sort of confused—“I’ll take you.” He had proceeded to charge me 80 USD for the half-hour drive.

But Joseph was just kind. I messaged him later to ask if he’d drive me to and from a tea farm a couple hours out from Nairobi, and I’d pay him for the day. Joseph pointed out landmarks to me as he drove and filled me in on the region’s background. The tea farm occupied multiple hills in this county known for its abundance of agricultural resources: dairy cows grazed on its fields; Nairobi drank from its water; avocado trees sprouted and dropped the giant, cantaloupe-sized avocados to the ground that I saw sold on Nairobi streets. People of all ages walked their wares down the street side, through tall grasses bordered by those characteristic sprawling Kenyan trees, snapping a switch at the donkeys they led pulling a big wooden cart, or with their bodies pitched forward carrying bundles of maize five people wide strapped to their backs. Dogs, goats, and chickens moved through the village with purpose, glancing at each other meaningfully before squeezing through the hole in a fence together, towards what was clearly a shared pre-planned destination. The village consisted of a few streets flanked intermittently by brick houses, more often than not painted MPESA green. (MPESA was the payment app ubiquitous in Kenya. I didn’t really understand why people used this thing, which required a real-life two-way transfer of several series of digits to authenticate each transaction, but it was the most base method of payment, accepted uniformly even where cash and card were not.) I wondered if MPESA had sponsored the stores, or the paint company. Once in a while we drove past a Kenyan flag, occasionally painted next to the Chinese flag. In Ethiopia, too, I’d seen Chinese lettering everywhere, announcements over the airport PA in Chinese following those in English and Amharic. Joseph told me the Chinese built the Kenyatta highway that we took out to the tea farm.

I guess that in some ways this whole scene felt idyllic and beautiful—especially to me, an outsider. You can imagine what you’d miss about growing up here. The muddy route through the tall forest, narrow trunks stretching to heaven, from the road to a fresh pounding waterfall; your older sister braiding your hair as you sit on a bench next to the street, chickens pecking at the ground between your feet; knowing everyone in the village, being sent by your family for a cut from the butcher’s shop that you’d seen him haul up from the slaughterhouse by cart just hours earlier.

At the same time you couldn’t detach all that from the corruption, the outright bribery tainting the government, the police officers firing live bullets into the crowd of protestors, the fact that my life here was so easy because despite my family being poor by American standards, the generosity of my school and my country made it possible for me to purchase practically any service I wanted here for a ridiculously small sum. My mom had just started doing Uber in the US. But if things didn’t work out at least she knew my brother could attend public school for free, and college for free too.

On the other hand: Joseph was doing Uber—and had agreed to taking me on this long ride out of the city, and waiting there, and taking me back—to put his son through high school. The school fees were due in two weeks and he did not know if he could come up with them in time. And was there even something waiting for his son on the other side? He told me: “This country is not going in a good direction. The leaders in the past invested in projects so the youth coming out of school would have jobs. Now there is none of that.”

You needed to interact with a million people to get anything done. This meant, in turn, that people assumed you welcomed human contact. Strangers touched my arm in the street to get my attention, flight attendants shook my body to wake me up when their snacks cart passed through. One night I went to a club called The Alchemist with Sana. While she read one of the books of short fiction she’d bought here, I downed two drinks and went on the dance floor. People grinded on strangers without bothering with a preliminary exchange of words; more than once I had a phone shoved in my face to enter my number on the screen, which I’d push away and shake my head to; people came, it was clear, to touch each other, and found it strange that I’d say no to a hand up my shirt. In front of me two Kenyan girls ground simultaneously, intensely, and with great spatial precision, upon two Italian brothers who kept looking at each other in giddy bewilderment. A woman tried grinding on me but I reciprocated only halfheartedly, afraid she’d feel I didn’t have a dick. After a few minutes of valiant effort on her part she turned around and asked me if I was gay.

It was fun, but only once. Twice, thrice, forever, would be too much. I missed dancing relatively unbothered in the US. There was a happy medium, I thought, between existing as an island and being considered such an inextricable part of the community that people assumed you were okay with any kind of touch just by virtue of your presence at the venue.

Sana and I left Nairobi late on the night I returned from the tea farm. I had fallen asleep briefly on the way back from the tea farm, my forehead against the passenger window. “Your people are a good people,” Joseph kept saying, referring to Chinese people. “They built this highway.” Our conversation had petered out at some point while the sky turned orange, and he’d turned down the radio, and let me sleep. It felt a bit wrong now to be sitting here counting out cash in the passenger seat next to him. He was looking straight ahead so I placed the cash on the glove box. “Good luck with everything,” I said. I was referring to his son’s school fees. “I had a good time. Thanks for driving me.”

“It was my first time going there, too,” he said. “A new part of Nairobi for me as well.” I went outside and waved and, a few hours later, left the city.
